02 — Problem & systemTwo verticals · many roles

Learning was connected. The experience wasn't.

Schedules, announcements, class links, and learning resources lived in different places. Students had to navigate between them to understand their academic day, while educators needed ways to create and manage sessions across different roles and learning environments.

I mapped the needs across both business verticals and established one shared calendar experience with role-specific navigation, permissions, and actions.

03 — Calendar & event systemWeek · Month

A familiar calendar, designed around learning.

I leaned on familiar calendar conventions instead of introducing a new interaction model. A desktop-first weekly and monthly calendar, clear date navigation, subject filtering, and recognizable event cards made the experience easier to understand at a glance.

The challenge was to support multiple event types and roles without making the interface feel complicated.

Both views share one event language, so moving between a week and a month changes the zoom level, not what anything means.

Why colour

Color gives every event a recognizable identity. Contests receive a distinct visual treatment because they are an important part of both learning ecosystems and can carry a different priority from routine classes.

04 — Interaction modelCard → details → action

One click closer to the next step.

The calendar card became an entry point into the learning experience, not just a reminder of when something happens.

Students can access relevant class information, join sessions, and find associated notes, questions, assignments, and quizzes. Educators and mentors have role-appropriate controls to create, edit, and manage events.

Special lectures and other academic activities can be surfaced to the intended audience, while students retain a simple, predominantly read-only scheduling experience.

What it meant

With approximately 2,300 weekly active users and 1,700 weekday daily active users, the calendar became a frequently used destination within an enrolled population of around 3,000 students.

Over 4–4.5 weeks I worked closely with developers through implementation, resolving design and engineering details together, and contributed to parts of the build through an AI-assisted workflow.

The most important design decision was treating the calendar as a gateway to the academic day, not another isolated feature in the LMS.

Reflection

Good product design doesn't always require a new mental model. Sometimes, it means bringing the right information together, making the next step obvious, and building a system that works for everyone without losing sight of its primary users.
